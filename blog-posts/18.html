VPN vs Proxy vs Tor: What's the Difference?

VPN, proxy, Tor — all three promise more privacy online, and all three get mixed up constantly. They’re not the same thing, and picking the wrong one can leave you exposed or pointlessly slow.

This guide explains each in plain language, compares them side by side, and tells you exactly when to use which. No technical background needed.

The Short Version

  • Proxy: reroutes one app’s traffic (usually your browser) through a middleman server. Fast and simple, but usually no encryption — anyone watching can still see your data.
  • VPN: encrypts all your device’s internet traffic and routes it through a secure server. The best all-rounder for privacy and security.
  • Tor: bounces your traffic through three random volunteer servers around the world for maximum anonymity. Very private, but very slow.

Think of it like sending a letter: a proxy is asking a friend to post it for you (they can read it); a VPN is sealing it in a tamper-proof envelope and using a courier; Tor is passing it through three strangers in disguise so nobody knows it came from you.

What Is a Proxy?

A proxy server sits between you and the website you want to visit. Your request goes to the proxy, the proxy forwards it, and the website sees the proxy’s address instead of yours.

Types you’ll hear about: - HTTP/HTTPS proxies — work inside your browser settings; only affect web browsing. - SOCKS proxies — more flexible; can handle other apps like torrent clients or games.

Pros: - Usually free or very cheap. - Easy to set up — often just an address and port number. - Can be fast, since there’s little or no encryption overhead. - Good for simple tasks like accessing a region-locked webpage.

Cons: - Most proxies don’t encrypt your traffic. Your ISP, the WiFi owner, or anyone snooping can still read what you send. - Only covers the app you configure — your other apps leak your real IP. - Free proxies are notoriously shady: many log everything and inject ads. - No protection against advanced tracking.

When to use one: low-stakes tasks where you just need a different IP address quickly — e.g., checking how a website looks from another country, or a developer testing geo-content. Not for anything sensitive.

What Is a VPN?

A VPN creates an encrypted tunnel between your device and a VPN server. Everything — every app, every website — travels through that tunnel. Outsiders (your ISP, the café WiFi owner, snoopers) see only scrambled data going to the VPN server.

Pros: - Encrypts all device traffic, not just the browser. - Hides your real IP address from websites. - Protects you on public WiFi (see our public WiFi safety guide). - Many providers have strict no-logs policies and have been independently audited. - Extras on paid plans: kill switch, ad-blocking, multiple devices.

Cons: - Good ones cost money (though trustworthy free tiers exist — see our free VPN guide). - Some speed loss from encryption and distance to the server. - You’re trusting the VPN company instead of your ISP — so choose a reputable one. - Streaming services often block VPN servers.

When to use one: everyday privacy and security — public WiFi, hiding browsing from your ISP, securing your connection while travelling, or remote work. For most people, most of the time, a VPN is the right answer.

What Is Tor?

Tor (The Onion Router) is a free, open-source network run by volunteers worldwide. Your traffic is wrapped in layers of encryption (like an onion) and passed through three random relays: entry → middle → exit. No single relay knows both who you are and what you’re doing.

Pros: - Strongest anonymity of the three — extremely hard to trace. - Free forever, run by a non-profit (the Tor Project). - Lets you access .onion sites (hidden services) that don’t exist on the regular web. - No single company to trust.

Cons: - Very slow — three hops around the world, run by volunteers. Forget HD video. - Some websites block Tor exit nodes or show endless CAPTCHAs. - The exit relay can see your unencrypted traffic — so still use HTTPS. - Can draw attention: some ISPs and networks flag Tor usage. - Not user-friendly for beginners (though the Tor Browser makes it one-click).

When to use one: when anonymity truly matters — journalists, activists, researchers, or anyone in a situation where being identified could be dangerous. Overkill (and painfully slow) for daily browsing.

Can You Combine Them?

Yes, and sometimes it makes sense:

  • VPN + Tor: connect to your VPN first, then open Tor Browser. Your ISP can’t see you’re using Tor, and you get Tor’s anonymity. Slower, but maximally private. (Tor Project’s official stance: this is fine but usually unnecessary for most people.)
  • VPN + proxy: mostly pointless — the VPN already covers everything the proxy would.
  • Tor over a proxy: adds complexity without much benefit for beginners.

For 99% of readers: just use a reputable VPN and don’t overthink it.

Common Myths, Busted

“A proxy is just a free VPN.” No. Without encryption, a proxy hides your IP from the website but exposes your activity to everyone between you and the proxy. Different tool, different job.

“Tor makes me 100% anonymous.” Nothing does. Tor is excellent, but browser fingerprinting, logging into personal accounts over Tor, or downloading files can still identify you. And remember: use HTTPS, because the exit node can see unencrypted traffic.

“VPNs make me completely untraceable.” A VPN hides your traffic from your ISP and your IP from websites, but if you log into Facebook, Facebook still knows it’s you. Privacy tools protect the connection, not your behaviour.

“Free proxies are fine for casual use.” Free proxies are among the riskiest privacy tools out there — many exist specifically to harvest data. If you need a free option, use a reputable free VPN tier instead.

Which One Should You Choose?

Ask yourself one question: what am I trying to protect?

  • “I want everyday privacy and security” → VPN. It’s the best balance of protection, speed, and ease. Start with a trusted free tier.
  • “I just need a different IP for one quick task” → Proxy. Fast and simple, but don’t send anything sensitive through it.
  • “I need maximum anonymity” → Tor Browser. Accept the slowness as the price of privacy.
  • “I’m on café WiFi and want to check my bank” → VPN (or better: use mobile data instead).

A Note on Responsible Use

All three are legitimate privacy tools used daily by businesses, journalists, and ordinary people. But privacy tools don’t make illegal activity legal, and they shouldn’t be used to evade lawful restrictions or harm others. Use them to protect your data — that’s what they’re for.

FAQ

Can I use a VPN and Tor at the same time? Yes — connect your VPN, then launch Tor Browser. It’s slower but adds a layer: your ISP won’t know you use Tor. Most beginners don’t need this.

Is Tor illegal? No. Tor is legal software built by a US non-profit and used by journalists, researchers, and privacy-conscious people worldwide. (What you do with any tool is what matters legally.)

Why is Tor so slow? Your data travels through three volunteer-run servers, possibly on different continents, with multiple layers of encryption. That distance and relay work costs speed.

Do I need a proxy if I have a VPN? Almost certainly not. A VPN already routes and encrypts all your traffic, which covers everything a proxy does — more securely.
